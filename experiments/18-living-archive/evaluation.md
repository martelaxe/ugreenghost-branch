# Experiment 18 — visual investigation and comparison

## Collection and what the investigator actually saw

Fifteen real historic photographs were downloaded as Wikimedia Commons thumbnails after checking the Commons API's individual `Public domain` labels. They include 1906 earthquake/fire/rescue/shelter scenes, an exposition scene from 1915 and a bridge damaged in the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake. The latter two are potentially misleading alternatives, not images of 1906. A source-grounded [catalog and attribution record](fixtures/attribution.md) lists each title, creator as cataloged, date, source page, credit, license label and SHA-256; [full API metadata](fixtures/private/source-catalog.json) is retained. This is a metadata-based rights assessment, not independent legal advice. The images are real; some are digitizations or crops, and Images 04 and 09 are two reproductions of the same Palace Hotel photograph.

The fresh original Codex process saw three actual labelled photographic contact sheets attached via `codex exec -i`, and read the neutral bibliographic catalog, contextual note and rights note. It was not given the later removal conditions or the private answer key. The preflight run [correctly described an attached image](preflight/vision-response.md), demonstrating visual input worked in that environment. Image sheets expose real image content but are resized: they do not establish accurate interpretation of fine details. The actual [original prompt and raw result](original/raw-run/) are retained. It chose 09 (hotel fire), 05 (rescue amid debris) and 14 (refugee camp); it explicitly treated the sequence as thematic, not the same people or a documented chronology. The model used catalog titles to identify the rescue and camp; this is visual inspection **plus textual evidence**, not image-only historical identification.

All seven main Codex processes (original plus six adaptations) were fresh, read-only, requested `gpt-6.1-sol` at low reasoning, and received actual contact sheets as image attachments. They exited successfully; their event logs, prompts, visual input hashes, final outputs and workspace hashes are preserved. The model service and configuration remained available, other host paths were not fully hidden, and network isolation was not independently established. The tasks were generated and evaluated by the same team and no real participant or historian independently judged the outputs. The [private answer key](fixtures/private/answer-key.md) was written after adaptation processes were launched but before results were read; its hash was not frozen before launch. The cases themselves were fixed in the runner before launch.

The historical note in the input asserted general shelter need but linked to a broad USGS landing page, rather than a specific shelter source. After the runs, the [National Park Service](https://www.nps.gov/places/000/1906-earthquake-cottages.htm) corroborated that residents were displaced and tents served as emergency shelter. This is **post-hoc corroboration**; the model did not read that page. It does not prove facts about specific people pictured.

All six resulting selections used known eligible image IDs and all input workspaces were unchanged ([mechanical audit](visual-review/mechanical-audit.json)). Review of central claims against the records found no obvious fabricated historical event or permission in the six outputs, but this was not an exhaustive independent historical audit. Visual wording such as “dark smoke” in 15 is an interpretation of a low-contrast image; the catalog, not pixels alone, supports the historical fire claim. Cataloged 05 and 14 identify rescue and shelter, but visual inspection does not establish individual identities or events. The metadata summary itself is not independently authenticated for every detail.

## Generic vs narrative-guided: fair but very small

For B and C, fresh G and N processes received byte-identical files, identical actual image attachments, original exhibit and ordinary careful visual-curation request. Both used the same JSON result structure and were instructed not to invent evidence. N additionally received explicit instructions to track each original narrative role, compare replacements against it, and preserve gaps where warranted. The common detailed output schema already asks both methods to explain their changes; this makes G competent and may reduce the observable difference between conditions. The author-designed JSON schema and renderer make explicit comparison and gap presentation possible regardless of method.

- **B:** Both G and N selected Image 02, removed the original rescue claim and retitled the exhibit *Fire, Ruins, Shelter*. Neither used the attractive unrelated 1915 fair or 1989 bridge. N offered a fuller alternative comparison, consistent with its longer instruction; the central decision was the same.
- **C:** Both correctly stated no supplied image established a shelter scene. G placed Image 02 in the third slot and changed the story to *Fire, Rescue, Ruins*. N kept the original shelter role visibly empty and described the missing visual evidence. Both options are allowed by the task and have different goals. The gap makes the loss more visible; no person has established it is preferable or more useful.

This is one response per method and condition, not a statistically meaningful benchmark. Generic and narrative-guided approaches both met the main truth constraint in these cases; the specialized prompt changed presentation in C but does not demonstrate superior understanding. [Blind packet](visual-review/blind/index.html) conceals treatment labels; its mapping is stored separately under `fixtures/private/` for later review. It has not been shown to external participants.
