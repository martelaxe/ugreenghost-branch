# Real creator evaluation: protocol and pending materials

**Status: not run.** No independent creators have participated; model evaluators are not people and their assessments are not user validation. Recruit approximately 3–5 adult photographers, designers, artists or makers who consent to evaluate fictional projects. No real private assets or personal data are needed. Ask participants to judge the proposals' claims, not to waive rights, render media or actually stage a display.

## Preparation

1. Use the actual, blinded outputs from trial 1 of each method for the same variant (prepared without choosing the best trial); anonymize model, method and workspace paths to `archive/`, and preserve source-file errors. Apply the same path-prefix normalization to both responses and tell participants that a broken citation is an error. Do not rewrite creative content. Provide the identical fictional source archive and request to everyone, with an explicitly bounded scope. The [review folder](review/) and `human-review/facilitator-mapping.json` preserve method mapping separately; share only a participant-case directory, not the entire experiment folder; the facilitator must not reveal it to participants until all ratings are locked.
2. Give participants a distinct randomized report order. For 3 participants use XY, YX, XY; for 4 use XY, YX, XY, YX; for 5 use XY, YX, XY, YX, XY. X/Y are neutral report labels, not scenario labels; tell each participant which one to read first. Randomize which method is first before order assignment and use a counterbalancing random seed logged outside participant materials. For exploratory breadth, repeat with another scenario if time permits; do not let one participant judge variants in a way that leaks intended answers. If showing more than one trial per method, label and balance this explicitly.
3. Ask the creator to read the original sources relevant to permissions. Explain that the archive and venue are fictional; distinguish no public-use permission, not-yet-known rights and provisional written permission. The product is a plan, not legal advice. Allow a 'neither' response throughout.

## Participant form

- Which proposal, if any, would you genuinely pursue? A / B / neither. Why?
- Which contains a possibility you personally had not already considered? A / B / both / neither. Describe it.
- Which better fits the time, budget and materials actually documented? A / B / tie / neither. What is missing?
- Which more accurately handles restrictions and uncertain rights? A / B / tie / neither. Point to source evidence.
- Which gives a clearer safe next action? A / B / tie / neither. What would you do first?
- Would you use a NAS feature like this with your own authorized material? Yes / maybe / no. What would need to change?
- What felt generic, unrealistic, surprising, unsafe, or unnecessary? Free text.
- Which citations or factual claims could you not verify? Free text.

Record responses verbatim or with consented notes, actual task time if measured, source access and whether any idea was already familiar. Collect no names beyond participant codes, contact information or creative work unless separately consented. Report all non-responses and any facilitator involvement.

## Interpretation

A 3–5 person convenience pilot can surface problems and support a next decision; it cannot measure market demand or establish statistically reliable superiority. Distinguish unexpected to this participant from objectively novel. One unsafe permission claim, invented evidence or unresolvable source is a serious failure even if a participant likes the idea. Success for an early qualitative pilot requires at least one person articulating a concrete use and correct restriction handling; a wider preference claim requires more balanced participants and further replication. Pre-register a separately designed, paired study before claiming a quantitative gain. Ask for explicit consent before any external contact or sending messages; no messages were sent here.
